# NCL CTF Reflections

This repository documents my competitive CTF experience, preparation methodology, and reflections on what I learned through participation in the National Cyber League (NCL) and other competitions.

> **Note on NCL policy:** In accordance with NCL guidelines, this repository does not include specific challenge solutions or writeups for NCL challenges. Reflections focus on methodology, skill development, and lessons learned.

---

## My CTF Journey

My curiosity about computers started early, but cybersecurity clicked for me specifically in high school when I started watching DEFCON and Black Hat talks on YouTube. What struck me about those talks wasn't the complexity — it was the underlying philosophy: to make a system behave differently than it was designed to, you first have to understand exactly how it was designed to work. That idea has stayed with me ever since.

For most of my career I've worked on the hardware side — data centers, physical infrastructure, servers, and networking equipment. Cybersecurity has been something I've been building toward deliberately through SANS coursework, certifications, and a home lab. CTF competitions became the place where I could pressure-test those skills against real problems. Digging deep into the software side after years on the hardware side has been one of the more exciting challenges I've taken on — and genuinely one of the more enjoyable ones.

## NCL Spring 2026

### Preparation: Practice Game

Before the Spring 2026 season, I spent approximately two months working through the NCL Practice Game as a structured preparation exercise. My goal wasn't to score — it was to understand where I was strong, where I had gaps, and what I needed to develop before the season started.

I treated each challenge as a documentation exercise: recording my initial approach, the rabbit holes I went down, where I got stuck, and how I ultimately arrived at a solution. I also used AI as a learning accelerator — not to produce answers, but to understand _why_ a command was structured a certain way, or to clarify a concept I hadn't worked with before. That combination of hands-on struggle followed by structured reflection was the most effective learning method I found throughout this process.

If a challenge took ten hours, it took ten hours. The time pressure wasn't the point — the learning was. Honestly, the practice game was my favorite part of the whole season for exactly that reason. With the clock off, every problem became a puzzle worth sitting with rather than a decision about where to spend limited time.

The team game demonstrated something different than individual skill — it demonstrated the value of coordinated effort under pressure. Top 3.4% doesn't come from any one person performing well. It requires knowing who takes what challenge, communicating partial progress without slowing each other down, and trusting teammates to close things out while you move on. That workflow mirrors what an effective SOC team or incident response unit has to operate like when the pressure is on.
